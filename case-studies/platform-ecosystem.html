Turning a closed product into a platform three audiences can trust

Defining the trigger-action journeys and partner-prioritization research behind a multi-quarter platform roadmap spanning end-users, IT admins, and third-party developers — three audiences with three different incentives.

TeamGoogle Workspace Platform
My roleSenior UX Researcher, research lead
Timeline2022 – 2024
Methods Trigger-action CUJ mapping Partner-prioritization survey Third-party developer interviews Cross-org roadmap workshops
Scale20+ PMs · 560% HaTS coverage growth

Context

Google Chat and Workspace were moving from a single-sided product — one user, one interface — to a three-sided ecosystem: end-users automating their own work, IT admins governing what could run inside their organization, and third-party developers building on top of the platform. Each group had different incentives, and product bets that pleased one group routinely broke trust with another. Research needed to hold all three perspectives at once, not optimize for whichever one shouted loudest in the roadmap meeting.

My role

I shaped the multi-quarter platform roadmap across 20+ product managers by defining trigger-action critical user journeys (CUJs) for workflow automation and third-party extensibility, and fielded the quantitative research that prioritized which partner integrations — think IT service management and work-management tools — were actually worth building first.

Approach

I mapped the end-to-end journeys of the people building automations and the people who benefited from them — who, it turned out, usually weren't the same person. That revealed a coordination problem hiding inside what looked, on paper, like a single-user feature: a "workflow builder" is actually a small, informal team the moment more than one person depends on what it does.

On the developer side, I interviewed third-party partners to understand what actually motivates someone to build on a platform versus walk away — separating genuine business-incentive friction (market size, distribution) from technical-cost friction (documentation, API reliability), which product teams tend to conflate.

To replace internal debate with evidence, I fielded a structured prioritization survey across candidate integrations, ranking which partnerships were worth building first. In parallel, I partnered with the platform's measurement team to scale the developer-satisfaction (HaTS) program from covering a handful of APIs to the platform's full surface area — a 560% increase in API coverage — so the org could track platform health continuously instead of one survey at a time.

Key insight

Developers didn't need more features from the platform — they needed proof the platform would still be there, documented and reliable, a year from now. Feature-parity anxiety and thin documentation turned out to be bigger adoption blockers than any single missing capability.

Impact

20+ PMsAligned on a shared trigger-action CUJ framework used to scope the multi-quarter automation roadmap.
560%Growth in API coverage for the platform's developer-satisfaction (HaTS) measurement program — from 5 to 33 APIs.
Survey-rankedPartner-integration priorities replaced internal debate with a quantitative model of what to build first.

Reflection

Platform research means designing for the negotiation between audiences at once — you're rarely testing a single screen, you're testing whether an ecosystem's incentives actually line up. The CUJ framework mattered less as a research artifact and more as a shared language that let PMs, engineers, and partners argue about the same thing.
