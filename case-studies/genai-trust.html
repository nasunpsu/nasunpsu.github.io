Grounding a generative AI product in evidence, from zero to GA

Leading end-to-end UX research for a new multimodal AI creation tool — from open-ended exploration to the Evals rubric and in-product measurement that governed the General Availability launch decision.

TeamGoogle Workspace — GenAI Productivity
My roleSenior UX Researcher, research lead
Timeline2024 – 2026
Methods Exploratory qualitative research Evals rubric design In-product HaTS instrumentation Executive research synthesis
Related publication"Gemini at Work," ACM DIS 2025

Context

Google was building a new multimodal AI creation tool — a generative product spanning both consumer and business tiers, later announced at Google I/O 2026. Leadership wanted research embedded from the earliest exploratory work all the way through the General Availability decision, not brought in after the product had already taken shape. The risk was familiar to anyone who's watched a generative AI feature ship on hype instead of evidence: a model that's technically impressive but doesn't survive contact with how people actually create and edit things.

My role

I led UX research for the product across its full lifecycle: open-ended exploratory work to define who the tool was really for and what "good" looked like for them, evaluative research that shaped the interaction model as it evolved, and — critically — the measurement infrastructure the team used to decide when the product was actually ready for General Availability.

Approach

I started before there was a clear product to test. Foundational research mapped how full-time creators and knowledge workers actually built and edited visual and written content today — where existing tools helped, and where AI-generated output created more cleanup work than it saved. That surfaced the workflow friction points a good generative tool would need to solve, rather than the ones that were easiest to build a demo around.

Because "does this feel useful" is notoriously unreliable as a self-reported metric for AI output, I designed a structured Evals rubric — a repeatable way for the team to score model output against real user needs instead of internal taste — and paired it with in-product HaTS (Happiness Tracking Survey) instrumentation, so we could watch sentiment move in near-real time as the model and interface changed release over release.

In parallel, I ran a companion research thread on how knowledge workers actually perceive productivity gains from generative AI more broadly. That work became a value framework that went beyond simple "time saved" math — and turned into a peer-reviewed paper, Gemini at Work (ACM DIS 2025), which reframed how the team argued internally for enterprise GenAI investment.

Key insight

People didn't lose trust in the generative tool because of raw output quality — they lost trust when the system's behavior was unpredictable: an edit that touched more than they'd asked for, or a result they couldn't trace back to anything they'd said. The biggest lever wasn't a better model. It was making AI behavior legible and controllable.

Impact

GA readinessEvals rubric + HaTS instrumentation became the team's shared source of truth for the General Availability go/no-go decision.
Consumer + BusinessResearch spanned both product tiers, surfacing where their needs diverged early enough to shape the roadmap, not just the launch copy.
1 peer-reviewed paperThe productivity-value framework became ACM DIS 2025 research, reshaping how the org pitched GenAI investment beyond time-savings.

Reflection

The clearest lesson from this project: in a 0-to-1 AI product, a research team's job isn't only to find problems — it's to build the measurement infrastructure so the rest of the org can see the same evidence you do, long after you've moved on to the next open question. A good Evals rubric outlives any single research readout.
